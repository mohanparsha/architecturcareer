Before you start

Print and cut

  • Use Print this pack for everything, or the button beside a week for that week alone. Choose A4, portrait, 100 per cent scale and default margins, and print on one side only.
  • Black and white is enough: nothing depends on colour and nothing is shaded.
  • Cards come four to a page. Cut once across and once down, along the dashed lines.
  • Every card carries its week, card number, tier and title, so a mixed pile can be sorted again.
  • Spare spaces on a page are blank cards for a scenario of your own. Each week’s note says how many copies to make.

Group the students

  • Use the group size the curriculum gives for the week. The note repeats it.
  • Form groups by counting off or by seating row, not by friendship or marks, and change them every week or two.
  • Give each group a reader, a timekeeper, a note-taker and a reporter, and rotate the jobs so that the same voices do not always lead.
  • In a large class, run two circuits with a second set of cards instead of making the groups bigger.

Ground rules for discussion

Read these aloud in the first week and keep them on the wall.

  1. We talk about the people on the card, not about people in this room.
  2. Nobody has to share anything about their own life, family or home. Anyone may say ‘pass’.
  3. One person speaks at a time. We disagree with ideas, not with people.
  4. No names and no stories about classmates or teachers, here or afterwards.
  5. The cards have no single right answer. Reasons matter more than verdicts.
  6. If a card is upsetting, anyone may ask quietly for a different one or step out, without giving a reason.

Safeguarding, for the teacher

  • Every card is fiction: the people and events are made up, and real towns only give a setting.
  • Never stage an incident or mislead students. Every role-play is announced, uses volunteers and lets anyone opt out without losing marks; private brief cards are a game rule the whole class is told about.
  • Never ask students about their own family, income, caste, religion, health or relationships, never let a card be turned on a real student, and never record students on audio or video.
  • If a student discloses something worrying: listen calmly, do not ask for details in front of others, do not promise secrecy, follow the school’s child protection policy and tell its designated person the same day. Find out who that is before Week 1.
  • Child Helpline: 1098. It runs under the Government of India’s Mission Vatsalya scheme (Ministry of Women and Child Development) and is linked to the emergency number 112.

Week 1 · facilitator note

Week 1: Intelligence over force

Aim. Students practise changing the shape of a problem before reaching for force, complaint or giving up: who else is involved, what each side wants, what is truly fixed and what is only assumed.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for 3 to 4 locked-scenario cards at different difficulty levels, one per team of four. Cards 1 to 4 are these, easiest first.
  • Tier 2. It asks for a private brief card for each of four roles in a made-up school dispute over the only playing field: the senior sports team, a junior club, a mediator and a school-magazine reporter. Cards 5 to 10 are the scene, the four briefs and a card setting out the four endings.
  • Tier 3. Students write their own scenario card in the teach-back lab, so none is supplied. Let them use cards 1 to 4 as a model.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 15-minute simulation slot, teams of four)

  1. 1 minHand one card to each team. Give the higher levels to teams that want a harder problem.
  2. 2 minTeams read silently and underline everything that is locked.
  3. 7 minEach team lists at least three ways forward that respect every lock, then chooses one.
  4. 4 minThree teams report in about a minute each: the idea, and which lock it works around.
  5. 1 minName the move each team made, for example ‘you changed who was at the table’.

Copies. One copy of the Tier 1 sheet for every four teams. Tier 2: one copy of both sheets per team of four.

Follow-up questions

  • Which lock did your team most want to break, and why that one?
  • Who in the scenario has influence that nobody has asked for yet?
  • What could go wrong with your idea, and what would you do then?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Separates what is fixed from what is only assumed.
  • Asks what the other side wants or fears, and offers something.
  • Brings in a third person, or changes the time or place, instead of escalating.
  • On card 3, puts the wishes of the student being mocked first.

Tier 2: running the negotiation. Tell the class openly that each role holds a private brief the others have not read, and that every brief will be read out at the end. That is a rule of the game, not a trick. Read card 5 aloud (1 minute), hand out cards 6 to 9 and give card 10 to whoever keeps notes (2 minutes), negotiate (15 minutes), then debrief (10 minutes): each role reads its brief aloud and the team says what would have changed had they known it earlier.

Link to the story. Like this week’s story, each card rewards the team that changes the problem rather than the team that pushes hardest against it.

Safeguarding. Card 3 is about mockery in a group chat: keep it about the people on the card, and do not ask whether anything similar has happened to anyone present. The Tier 2 dispute is invented; do not recast it with the school’s real teams or captains. A Tier 3 teach-back with younger students runs with a teacher present. If a student discloses something worrying, listen, do not ask for details in front of others and do not promise secrecy. Follow the school’s child protection policy and tell its designated person the same day. Child Helpline: 1098.

The four endings

Outcomes card for the note-taker

Your negotiation must finish in one of four ways. Exclusion: the junior club gets no time on the field. Compromise: both sides give something up and both agree. Week 2 · facilitator note

Week 2: Empathy as decision intelligence

Aim. Students weigh personal benefit against the good of a group, and notice that the way a leader decides matters as much as the decision.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for 3 progressively harder dilemma cards for Leader’s Choice, played in three rounds by groups of five. Cards 1 to 3 follow one student leader through three rounds, each costing her more than the last.
  • Tiers 2 and 3. The Tier 2 simulation uses stakeholder briefs rather than cards, and Tier 3 students design their own materials. Neither is covered in this version.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 14-minute simulation slot, groups of five)

  1. 4 minRound 1. Hand out card 1 only. Read (1 minute), discuss (2), each group states its decision in one line (1).
  2. 4 minRound 2. Hand out card 2 and repeat. Groups add to their consequence map: who gains, who loses.
  3. 5 minRound 3. Hand out card 3. Allow an extra minute: this round has no comfortable answer.
  4. 1 minEach group names the round it found hardest and says why in one sentence.

Copies. One copy of the sheet per group, cut, so that the cards can be handed out one round at a time.

Follow-up questions

  • Did your group’s answer change as the cost to Tanvi rose? At which round?
  • Is a sacrifice still generous if nobody knows about it?
  • When is saying no the responsible choice for a leader?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Names everyone affected, not only the obvious people.
  • Separates giving something up from deciding fairly.
  • Sees that asking the team is itself a decision.
  • Does not treat self-sacrifice as automatically right, or pretend round 3 has a costless answer.

Link to the story. This week’s story puts the question at its most extreme; the cards bring it down to the size of a school team so that students can argue about where the line falls.

Safeguarding. Card 2 mentions students who have never been on a school trip. Keep it about Bhavna and Omkar: do not ask who in the class can or cannot afford trips, or what students themselves have given up for others.

Week 3 · facilitator note

Week 3: Know when not to interfere

Aim. Students trace how one small, unconsidered action multiplies, and find the point in the chain where a pause or a question would have changed everything.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for 3 cascading scenario prompts with invented names for the Mapping Lab, and gives a rumour forwarded about someone in another school as an example. Cards 1 to 3 are three such prompts; card 1 is the rumour. It also lists risk alert cards and pledge cards: those are blank slips that students fill in themselves, so plain paper cut to size is enough.
  • Tiers 2 and 3. The Tier 2 simulation works from a policy proposal rather than cards, and Tier 3 students design their own workshop. Neither is covered in this version.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 15-minute mapping lab, groups of four)

  1. 4 minCard 1. Read (1 minute), then draw the chain: the first action, at least three knock-on effects, and who is affected at each link.
  2. 4 minCard 2, the same way. Ask groups to mark the link where stopping would have been cheapest.
  3. 4 minCard 3, the same way. This one also asks what to do after the damage is done.
  4. 3 minEach group shares one chain and the single question the character should have asked first.

Copies. One copy of the sheet per group. If time is short, give each group one card and let three groups report.

Follow-up questions

  • What did the person not know at the moment they acted?
  • Which link in the chain was the cheapest place to stop?
  • Is staying out always the safe choice? When is stepping in right?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Shows at least three links, each with a person affected.
  • Separates what was intended from what happened.
  • Finds the question that was skipped: is it true, whose is it, do I understand how it works?
  • Suggests repair as well as prevention, and does not conclude ‘never get involved’.

Link to the story. Each card is this week’s story in modern dress: someone acts on a thing they do not understand, and the cost lands further away than they expected.

Safeguarding. Card 1 is about a rumour, and every name on the cards is invented. Stop the discussion if it turns to a real rumour or a real classmate. The curriculum’s personal reflection this week is written privately and is not read out. If a student discloses something worrying, listen, do not ask for details in front of others and do not promise secrecy. Follow the school’s child protection policy and tell its designated person the same day. Child Helpline: 1098.

Week 4 · facilitator note

Week 4: Reason beats appearance

Aim. Students separate what is shown from what is merely suggested, and build a verdict on evidence rather than on how a person seems.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for a simple case brief with 3 pieces of evidence and 2 red herrings, one copy per team, for a courtroom of two teams, with the case and every name in it invented. Cards 1 to 3 are three such briefs of rising difficulty: use one per session.
  • Teacher’s key. Card 1: items 1, 2 and 4 bear on the question; 3 and 5 are red herrings. Card 2: items 1, 2 and 4; red herrings 3 and 5. Card 3: items 1, 2 and 3; red herrings 4 and 5. Do not tell the teams how many red herrings there are.
  • Tiers 2 and 3. The Tier 2 trial needs a longer case file than a card can carry, and Tier 3 students design their own process. Neither is covered in this version.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 16-minute courtroom, two teams and three judges)

  1. 1 minChoose one case. Give the same card to both sides and name a judge, or a bench of three. Anyone who plays the accused or a witness uses the name on the card.
  2. 4 minEach side sorts the five items into ‘bears on the question’ and ‘does not’, using the claim versus evidence sheet.
  3. 3 minThe prosecution argues.
  4. 3 minThe defence argues.
  5. 2 minThe judges ask one question of each side.
  6. 3 minThe judges give a verdict with reasons. ‘Not shown’ is an allowed verdict.

Copies. Two copies of the chosen card for each side and one for the judges.

Follow-up questions

  • Which item felt convincing but proved nothing? Why did it feel convincing?
  • What one further fact would you ask for before deciding?
  • Is ‘we cannot tell yet’ a fair verdict, or a way of avoiding one?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Sorts the items before arguing.
  • Sees that a person’s marks, mood or past scolding are not evidence.
  • Notices when two items contradict each other (card 2) or point to a third explanation (card 3).
  • Separates carelessness from dishonesty.

Link to the story. As in this week’s story, the fair answer comes from testing the claim rather than trusting how things look.

Safeguarding. The cases and names are invented. Never rerun the court on a real incident, and let no student play an accused person under their own name. The verdict is about the argument, not about a student.

Week 10: Think in systems, not impulses

Aim. Students plan with the other players in mind: they anticipate, choose partners on purpose, and adapt when conditions change.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for War Game instruction cards for 3 rounds: basic competition, then an alliance option, then a surprise disruption. Cards 1 to 3 are those rounds, set at a school fete.
  • Tier 2. It asks for hidden event cards that disrupt each of the four rounds of the Alliance Game, and suggests drought, rebellion and a trade route closure. Cards 4 to 7 are four event cards. Unlike the rest of the pack they are set in the game’s imaginary kingdoms, not in present-day life.
  • Tier 3. Students design their own simulation, so none is supplied.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 16-minute war game, four or five teams)

  1. 4 minRound 1. Read card 1 (1 minute); teams decide and write down their three choices (3).
  2. 1 minPut every team’s choices on the board.
  3. 5 minRound 2. Read card 2 (1 minute); teams negotiate (4). Agreements must be written.
  4. 4 minRound 3. Read card 3 and let teams respond.
  5. 2 minEach team says whether it kept its agreement, and why.

Copies. One Tier 1 sheet per team, cut, with one card handed out per round. Tier 2: one copy for the teacher only.

Follow-up questions

  • What did you assume the other teams would do, and were you right?
  • Why that partner, and on those terms?
  • When conditions changed, what was your promise worth?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Thinks about the other teams’ likely moves before choosing its own.
  • Does something different from its rivals instead of copying them.
  • Makes alliance terms explicit, and fair enough to last.
  • Weighs the gain from breaking a promise against being remembered for it.

Scoring. The curriculum lists play tokens. The cards work without them: if you do score, reward reasoning that took the other teams into account, not the loudest stall.

Tier 2: running the event cards. Tell the teams at the start that an event will be read out before every round, then keep cards 4 to 7 face down and read one per round. The curriculum’s 18 minutes allows about four and a half minutes a round. Its rules allow an alliance to be broken inside the game: say so beforehand, and make clear in the debrief that what happens in the game stays in the game.

Link to the story. This week’s text is about reading the whole field of rivals, neighbours and allies before moving; the cards make students read that field for a stall or a kingdom, and then test it.

Safeguarding. Allies and rivals are always groups (stalls, kingdoms, houses, clubs, teams), never named classmates. Watch that alliances do not become a way of leaving the same students out every round, and change the teams if they do.

Week 11: Build systems that work

Aim. Students design an organisation as parts that depend on one another, and watch how a failure in one part travels to the rest.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • Tier 1. The curriculum asks for 7 role cards per team, one for each pillar of the model, and 2 stress-test scenarios (‘your ally betrays you’, ‘your treasury runs out’). Cards 1 to 7 are the role cards. Cards 8 and 9 are those two stress tests, and card 10 adds a third (a key person leaves) from the Tier 2 list of crises.
  • Names of the pillars. The tag on each role card gives the curriculum’s name for the pillar and its plain gloss. The curriculum page, which also calls them elements or limbs, explains the model and gives its source.
  • Tiers 2 and 3. Tier 2 teams can use the same role cards and all three stress tests. Tier 3 students design their own workshop.

Running the cards in one period (Tier 1, the 18-minute build, teams of seven, then a 4-minute stress test)

  1. 1 minEach team chooses the organisation it will build.
  2. 1 minDeal cards 1 to 7, one to each member.
  3. 6 minEach role-holder answers the ‘decide and note down’ items on their own card.
  4. 8 minThe team draws its system map on chart paper: seven pillars, with arrows showing who depends on whom.
  5. 2 minCheck that every pillar has at least one arrow in and one arrow out.

Copies. One copy of the two role-card sheets (cards 1 to 7) per team, and one copy of the stress-test sheet for the teacher. For the stress test, read out one of cards 8 to 10 and give teams four minutes to trace it and answer.

Follow-up questions

  • Which pillar did every other pillar depend on?
  • Which pillar had nobody checking it?
  • What did the stress test show that the map did not?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Gives every role a clear job and a back-up.
  • Draws dependence in both directions.
  • Puts a check on the leader and on the treasury, and writes things down.
  • After the stress test, changes the design instead of blaming a person.

Link to the story. This week’s text treats an organisation as parts that stand or fall together; the cards ask students to build one small enough to see whole.

Safeguarding. Card 10 removes a role chosen at random. Present it as the role leaving, never as a comment on the student who holds it. When students talk about their own school or club, the weak pillar is a function or an office, never a named teacher or student.

Week 12: Apply the full stack

Aim. Students bring a story, a principle and a workable proposal together on a real problem.

What the curriculum asks for, and what is here

  • All tiers. The curriculum does not ask for scenario cards this week. It asks the teacher to prepare a list of 10 suggested capstone topics for teams that struggle to choose, and the curriculum page lists its own capstone options. Cards 1 to 4 are four starter problems for such teams: four, not ten. A team that already has its own topic does not need a card.

Running the cards in one period (Period 1, the 8 minutes for team formation and topic selection)

  1. 2 minTeams with a topic begin. Teams without one read the four cards and choose.
  2. 3 minThe team agrees which earlier week’s story fits the problem, and why that one.
  3. 3 minThe team writes the problem in one sentence, in its own words, then carries on with the curriculum’s Period 1 plan.

Copies. One copy of the sheet for every two or three teams.

Follow-up questions

  • Why this story and not another?
  • Who has to agree before your proposal can happen?
  • What would you measure after a month?

What a thoughtful response tends to do

  • Chooses the story for its principle, not for a surface likeness.
  • Proposes something students could run themselves.
  • Names who is affected and who decides.
  • Includes a small first step and a way to check it, and punishes or exposes nobody.

Link to the story. The capstone has no single story of its own: each card asks the team to decide which of the earlier weeks’ stories earns its place.

Safeguarding. Card 4 is about exclusion and mockery. Topics are about the school or community in general, never a named person. No proposal may involve identifying or exposing individual students, and no team should survey classmates about personal matters without the school’s permission. If a student discloses something worrying, listen, do not ask for details in front of others and do not promise secrecy. Follow the school’s child protection policy and tell its designated person the same day. Child Helpline: 1098.
